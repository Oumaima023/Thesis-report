
\chapter{Design and Data Architecture of the Proposed OEE Framework}
\label{chap:oee-framework-design}

\section{Introduction}

The previous chapter identified several limitations in applying classical \gls{oee} to autonomous yard logistics. These limitations include variable mission durations, heterogeneous interruptions, human supervision, and \gls{odd}-related restrictions. Classical \gls{oee} does not explicitly represent these characteristics. Its concepts must therefore be adapted to produce meaningful results for autonomous transport operations.

This chapter presents the \gls{oee}-based framework developed for autonomous yard logistics. It preserves the classical relationship between Availability, Performance, and Quality. It also adapts the measurement unit, time hierarchy, loss classification, and interpretation of these factors. The main contributions include a \textbf{transfer-based measurement unit, a six-level time hierarchy, an \gls{odd}-aware time-accounting method, and a decomposition of Performance into dispatch utilisation and execution speed}.

The chapter also introduces a transfer-outcome taxonomy and complementary indicators for reliability, supervision, throughput, environmental influence, and process capability. It assesses the availability of the industrial data required for implementation and defines a canonical data model for structuring this information. These elements provide the basis for the implementation and validation presented in the following chapter.
\section{Design Principles of the Adapted OEE Framework}
\label{sec:pwd_context}

Classical \gls{oee} was developed to evaluate fixed manufacturing assets. It measures effectiveness through equipment availability, operating speed, and product quality. Applying it directly to autonomous yard-logistics vehicles is not straightforward because their operating conditions differ from those of conventional production systems. In this work, I preserve the mathematical structure of classical OEE while adapting its concepts to autonomous transport operations.

I developed the framework around five main design decisions. First, I selected the transfer as the main unit of productive output, while retaining missions and mission phases for diagnostic analysis. Second, I introduced scenario- and phase-dependent nominal durations to account for the variability of autonomous transport operations. Third, I decomposed Performance into dispatch utilisation and execution speed. Fourth, I separated ODD-related exclusions from technical downtime through a sequential time hierarchy and overlap-correction rules. Finally, I distinguished transfer outcomes from autonomy and supervision indicators. \textbf{These decisions adapt OEE to the variable and constrained nature of autonomous transport operations.}

Based on these decisions, I maintain the relationship \textbf{$\mathrm{OEE} = A \times P \times Q$} while adapting the interpretation of each factor to autonomous yard logistics. I also ensure that operating time and operational losses are attributed completely and without overlap. The resulting framework remains mathematically consistent while providing detailed information about autonomous system performance.

\section{The OEE Measurement Unit}
\label{sec:oee-measurement-unit}
I selected the transfer as the primary OEE measurement unit because it represents the complete logistics service delivered by the vehicle. In classical OEE, the productive output is usually represented by a manufactured product. In autonomous yard logistics, however, the system provides a transport service rather than producing a physical product. The transfer therefore serves as the natural accounting unit. It represents one complete transport order that moves a swap body from a source location to a destination location.

As introduced in Section~\ref{sec:industrial-operating-context}, the operational process has three levels: transfer, mission, and mission phase. I evaluate OEE primarily at the transfer level, while retaining missions and mission phases for diagnostic analysis. This structure preserves the complete operational context. It also allows losses identified at transfer level to be traced to the underlying execution sequence. Figure~\ref{fig:hierarchical_organisation} illustrates this measurement hierarchy.

\section{The Six-Level Time Hierarchy}
\label{sec:six-level-time-hierarchy}

I developed a six-level time hierarchy to separate scheduling losses, planned exclusions, ODD restrictions, unplanned downtime, performance losses, and quality losses. Each level is obtained from the previous one by subtracting a specific category of loss or exclusion. This sequential structure assigns every part of the operating period to one category. It also prevents double counting and keeps the OEE factors mathematically consistent. The overlap-correction mechanism for ODD exclusions and downtime is detailed in Section~\ref{sec:weather-data-processing-and-odd-integration}.

The hierarchy starts with total calendar time $T_t$. This represents the complete evaluation period. I remove non-operating periods, such as nights, weekends, and scheduled non-operating days, to obtain Planned Operating Time $T_o$.

I then remove planned stoppages, such as maintenance, refuelling, and system preparation. I also remove periods during which operation is not expected because the vehicle is outside its Operational Design Domain. The remaining time is Required Time $T_r$. Unplanned interruptions during Required Time are then removed to obtain Available Time $T_a$.

Next, I subtract performance losses to obtain Performance-Adjusted Time $T_n$. This value represents the nominal-speed equivalent duration of all qualifying started transfers. Finally, I subtract quality losses to obtain Useful Time $T_u$. This value represents the nominal-speed equivalent duration of successfully completed transfers.

The resulting decomposition is expressed as:

\[
T_t \xrightarrow{-\text{non-operating}} T_o
\xrightarrow{-\text{ODD exclusions}} T_r
\xrightarrow{-\text{unplanned downtime}} T_a
\xrightarrow{-\text{performance losses}} T_n
\xrightarrow{-\text{quality losses}} T_u
\]

Based on this hierarchy, I define the three OEE components as:

\[
A = \frac{T_a}{T_r}, \qquad P = \frac{T_n}{T_a}, \qquad Q = \frac{T_u}{T_n},
\]

Therefore:

\[
\mathrm{OEE} = A \times P \times Q = \frac{T_u}{T_r}.
\]

The proposed hierarchy differs from classical OEE in two important ways. First, Required Time $T_r$ excludes ODD-related periods. The vehicle is therefore not penalised for conditions under which autonomous operation is not permitted. Second, I calculate Performance and Quality using the nominal durations of qualifying and successful transfers. This preserves the relationship $\mathrm{OEE}=T_u/T_r$ and maintains mathematical consistency.




\section{Availability}

Availability measures the fraction of \textbf{Required Time} during which the autonomous vehicle is operationally available:

\[
A = \frac{T_a}{T_r} = \frac{T_r - \text{Unplanned Downtime}}{T_r}.
\]

I defined Required Time so that planned stoppages and ODD-related periods are removed before Availability is calculated. Since $T_r$ already excludes planned stoppages andperiods outside the Operational Design Domain (ODD) (Section~\ref{sec:weather-data-processing-and-odd-integration}), Availability reflects exclusively \textbf{unplanned downtime}. Consequently, ODD-related exclusions reduce the OEE denominator but do not affect the Availability factor. It ensures that the vehicle is not penalised for conditions under which autonomous operation is not permitted.

Unplanned downtime events are classified according to three complementary dimensions, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:unplanned_downtime}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Category} describes the nature of the interruption. Two principal categories are distinguished:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System faults}, corresponding to failures originating from the autonomous system itself, such as localisation failures, trajectory deviations, or low-level system errors.
    \item \textbf{Human interventions}, corresponding to operator-initiated interruptions, including manual vehicle takeover and client-requested transfer aborts.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Owner} identifies the responsible party (autonomous system or operations), enabling consistent attribution of losses and supporting root-cause analysis.
  \item \textbf{Failure status} distinguishes engineering failures from supervisory or operational events, providing the basis for the complementary reliability metrics presented in Section~\ref{subsec:reliability-and-process-quality-indicators}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Performance}
\label{sec:performance}

I decomposed Performance into the \gls{dur} and the \gls{esr}. This separation distinguishes unused Available Time from delays during mission execution.

Classical OEE evaluates Performance by comparing actual production speed with an ideal cycle time. This approach is not sufficient for autonomous yard logistics. Low Performance may result from two different causes. The vehicle may remain available without executing a mission. Alternatively, an assigned mission may take longer than its nominal duration.

To distinguish these causes, I define Performance as the product of two factors:

\[
P = \mathrm{DUR} \times \mathrm{ESR}.
\]

$T_e$ denote the total actual execution time of qualifying started transfers. Let $T_n$ denote the sum of their corresponding nominal durations.

I define the \textbf{Dispatch Utilisation Rate} as:

\[
\mathrm{DUR} = \frac{T_e}{T_a}.
\]

\gls{dur} measures the proportion of Available Time spent executing qualifying transport operations. It captures losses caused by a lack of orders, dispatch delays, waiting between tasks, and underutilisation of the vehicle.

I define the \textbf{Execution Speed Rate} as:

\[
\mathrm{ESR} = \frac{T_n}{T_e}.
\]

\gls{esr} compares the nominal duration of the executed work with its actual duration. It captures losses caused by slow navigation, waiting during execution, obstacle interactions, retries, and other delays that extend mission duration.

The decomposition preserves the original Performance factor:

\[
P = \frac{T_e}{T_a} \times \frac{T_n}{T_e} = \frac{T_n}{T_a}.
\]

This decomposition provides more diagnostic information than a single Performance ratio. A low \acrshort{dur} indicates that the available vehicle was not sufficiently utilised. A low \acrshort{esr} indicates that assigned work was executed more slowly than its nominal reference. These two causes can therefore be analysed separately.

Finally, scenario- and phase-dependent nominal durations reduce the limitations of the fixed-cycle assumption. Each transfer is compared with an appropriate operational reference rather than with one common cycle time.
\section{Quality}
I introduced a five-class transfer-outcome taxonomy to represent successful, manually assisted, aborted, cancelled, and unachievable operations.

In autonomous yard logistics, quality cannot be assessed through the conformity of a manufactured product. Instead, it is defined by the outcome of the transport operation. The proposed framework therefore introduces a five-class outcome taxonomy that distinguishes successful, unsuccessful, and excluded transport executions.

Transfers completed autonomously deliver the required transport service and are therefore considered successful. Aborted transfers and manually completed transfers consume operational resources without producing the intended outcome and consequently constitute quality losses. Cancelled and unachievable transfers are excluded because execution was never expected under valid operating conditions.

To capture different aspects of operational quality, two complementary indicators are defined.

The \textbf{time-based quality}

\[
Q_{\mathrm{time}} = \frac{T_u}{T_n}
\]

is used in the computation of OEE and preserves the fundamental relationship

\[
\mathrm{OEE} = A \times P \times Q = \frac{T_u}{T_r}.
\]

The \textbf{count-based quality}

\[
Q_{\mathrm{count}} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{successful}}}{N_{\mathrm{started}}}
\]

provides an operational view of mission success independent of execution duration. Reporting both indicators distinguishes losses caused by unsuccessful executions from those resulting from differences in mission duration or route complexity.

The differences between these indicators provide additional diagnostic information. The gap between \acrshort{gee} and \acrshort{oee} reflects the exclusions applied between Planned Operating Time $T_o$ and Required Time $T_r$, including planned stoppages and ODD-related exclusions within the operating window. The gap between \acrshort{teep} and \acrshort{gee} reflects the calendar time outside the planned operating schedule, such as nights, weekends, and other non-operating periods.


\section{ODD Exclusion Framework --- Weather ODD, Operational ODD, and Double-Counting Prevention}
\label{sec:odd-exclusion-framework}

I designed the ODD-aware time-accounting method to prevent environmental and operational restrictions from being classified as technical failures.

The Operational Design Domain (ODD), introduced in Section~\ref{sec:pwd_context}, defines the environmental and operational conditions under which the autonomous driving system is expected to perform its intended function. Periods in which the vehicle operates outside its ODD do not represent equipment unavailability; rather, operation is not required or permitted under those conditions. Therefore, ODD-excluded periods are removed from the Required Time $T_r$ definition instead of being treated as Availability losses.

Within the proposed framework, ODD exclusion is implemented by subtracting the corresponding excluded duration from the transition between Planned Operating Time $T_o$ and Required Time $T_r$. The framework distinguishes two complementary sources of ODD exclusion: weather-related constraints and operational constraints.

\subsection{Weather ODD}

Weather-based exclusions represent environmental conditions that exceed the operational limits defined for the autonomous vehicle. Hourly meteorological observations are evaluated against configurable thresholds stored in the \texttt{ODD\_Parameters} table.

\scriptsize

\label{tab:weather-odd-criteria}
\begin{tabular}{|p{2.4cm}|p{3.8cm}|p{5.3cm}|}
\hline
\textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Exclusion criterion} \\
\hline
Heavy rain & \texttt{precip\_threshold\_mm\_h} & Precipitation rate above threshold \\
\hline

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Each shift hour violating at least one weather constraint is classified as ODD-excluded, and its duration is stored, thereby reducing the Required Time $T_r$. When multiple weather constraints occur simultaneously, a fixed precedence rule (rain $>$ snow $>$ wind $>$ freezing $>$ fog) is applied only for event attribution and traceability.

\subsection{Operational ODD}

In addition to environmental constraints, autonomous yard operations are affected by external operational conditions that determine whether a valid mission can be executed. Examples include an unavailable swap body, a blocked destination position, or an incorrect swap body type assignment. These conditions prevent mission execution independently of the vehicle capability.

Each operational ODD exclusion is classified similarly, and the accumulated duration is stored after applying weather-based exclusions, with the total daily ODD exclusion capped at the planned shift duration.

The Required Time is therefore calculated as:

\[
T_r = T_o - T_{startup} - T_{refueling} - T_{SW/FW} - T_{planned\_maint} - T_{out\_of\_odd}
\]

where $T_{out\_of\_odd}$ represents the total duration excluded due to weather and operational ODD violations.

\subsection{Double-Counting Prevention}

I introduced overlap-correction rules to ensure that each period is assigned to only one exclusion or loss category.

ODD periods may overlap with downtime or with other operational exclusions. Without correction, the same duration could be removed more than once.

For example, a technical interruption may occur during an hour that has already been excluded because of weather. Deducting its full duration from Available Time would penalise the vehicle for a period during which operation was not required.

For each event, only the part outside an already excluded ODD period is retained:

\[
T_{effective} = T_{event} - T_{event \cap ODD}
\]

or equivalently:

\[
T_{effective} = T_{event}(1 - f_{overlap})
\]

where $f_{overlap}$ represents the fraction of the event duration overlapping with an already excluded ODD period.

This correction is applied to both unplanned downtime events and operational ODD events during the transfer-level time-hierarchy computation (Section~\ref{subsec:time-hierarchy-computation}), ensuring that every lost second is attributed to exactly one category within the time hierarchy. Consequently, ODD limitations affect the operational time requirement without artificially reducing Availability, preserving the physical interpretation of the OEE factors.

\section{Operational Deployment Indicators}

The proposed framework defines complementary indicators to explain the technical and operational causes behind the aggregate OEE value. These indicators cover reliability, execution robustness, human supervision, operational capacity, environmental influence, and execution-time capability. Where the required input data are available, they can be computed from the same canonical data structure used for OEE calculation.

\subsection{Engineering Reliability Metrics --- MTBF, MTTR, and Failure Rate}

As introduced in Section~\ref{sec:reliability_state_art}, MTBF, MTTR, and failure rate provide complementary information on system reliability and maintainability. Within the proposed framework, these indicators are used to distinguish the technical failure behaviour of the autonomous vehicle from the broader operational losses captured by OEE.

Only genuine technical or equipment failures are considered reliability events. Supervisory interventions, operational constraints, and ODD-related exclusions are excluded because they do not directly represent hardware or software failures.

For the proposed framework, MTBF is calculated using Available Time $T_a$ as the operational exposure time:

\[
\mathrm{MTBF} = \frac{T_a}{N_{\mathrm{fail}}}
\]

while MTTR represents the average repair or recovery duration associated with the detected failures:

\[
\mathrm{MTTR} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{fail}}} t_{\mathrm{recovery},i}}{N_{\mathrm{fail}}}.
\]

The corresponding failure rate is obtained as defined in Section~\ref{sec:reliability_state_art}:

\[
\lambda = \frac{1}{\mathrm{MTBF}}.
\]

These indicators complement OEE Availability by distinguishing whether technical losses result primarily from frequent failures or from prolonged recovery periods:

\subsection{Execution-time capability analysis}
\label{subsec:execution-time-capability-analysis}

In addition to execution success and operational throughput, the consistency of autonomous execution is evaluated through process capability analysis. While process-quality indicators describe \textbf{how} operations are executed, capability analysis evaluates \textbf{how consistently} execution duration remains within an expected range.

Execution-time capability is evaluated using transfer and mission durations relative to a tolerance band centred around the nominal duration:

\[
\mathrm{USL} = 1.3 \times T_{\mathrm{nominal}}
\]

\[
\mathrm{LSL} = 0.7 \times T_{\mathrm{nominal}}
\]

The capability indices are calculated as:

\[
C_p = \frac{\mathrm{USL} - \mathrm{LSL}}{6\sigma}
\]

\[
C_{pk} = \min\left(\frac{\mathrm{USL} - \mu}{3\sigma}, \frac{\mu - \mathrm{LSL}}{3\sigma}\right)
\]

where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ represent the mean and standard deviation of the measured execution durations.

The $C_p$ index evaluates the spread of execution times relative to the defined tolerance interval, whereas $C_{pk}$ additionally considers the deviation of the process mean from the nominal duration. Therefore, $C_{pk}$ can identify systematic slowdowns or accelerations even when execution variability remains limited.

Capability analysis is performed at two levels:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Transfer-scenario level:} evaluates end-to-end predictability from an operational and logistics perspective.
  \item \textbf{Mission-phase level:} evaluates the consistency of individual MP1–MP8 execution durations relative to their phase-specific nominal references.
\end{itemize}

Together, these complementary indicators extend the OEE framework from a pure effectiveness measurement toward a broader evaluation of autonomous system maturity, operational capacity, and deployment readiness.


\section{Industrial Data Assessment and OEE Input Availability}
\label{sec:industrial-data-sources-oee-input-availability}
I assessed the datasets used to implement the proposed framework and evaluated their suitability for OEE computation. This section presents the available industrial and environmental data sources. It then examines their temporal coverage, granularity, consistency, and completeness. Finally, it compares the information required by the OEE model with the information available in the source records.

This assessment has two objectives. First, it identifies the data constraints that motivate the processing and reconstruction methods used in the implementation pipeline. Second, it identifies the additional fields required for more reliable OEE computation in future deployments. I therefore use the real data to assess the feasibility of the proposed approach and define its data requirements. The data is not treated as a complete or controlled measurement campaign.
\subsection{Available Industrial Data Sources}
\label{subsec:available-industrial-data-sources}
The implementation uses two operational datasets: an automatic dataset export and a manual field log. These sources differ in structure, granularity, and content. The automatic dataset provides machine-generated information, while the field log adds operator-recorded context.
\subsubsection{Automatic dataset}
The automatic dataset covers two acquisition campaigns. Phase 1 ran from \textbf{11 December 2022 to 10 February 2023}. Phase 2 took place in \textbf{March 2023}. The dataset contains three linked levels:

\begin{itemize}
  \item 425 transfers with 99 attributes;
  \item 750 missions with approximately 38 attributes;
  \item 3,137 incidents with approximately 28 attributes.
\end{itemize}

Transfer records describe complete transport orders. They include retry, abort, error, soft-stop, disengagement, and manual-mode counters. They also contain operating-mode durations, travelled distances, and GPS information.

Mission records include the mission type, duration, distance, state, and error information. Incident records capture events such as soft stops, protective stops, disengagements, and transitions to manual operation.

\subsubsection{Manual field-log dataset}
The manual dataset contains \textbf{944} operator-recorded mission attempts across seven test weeks. Phase 1 includes \textbf{weeks 50, 2, 3, 5, and 6}. Phase 2 includes \textbf{weeks 10 and 11}. Each row represents one mission attempt and contains approximately 25 attributes.

These attributes cover identification, timing, outcome, human intervention, and environmental context. They include mission and scenario information, start and completion times, manoeuvre and catch-up durations, success or failure status, failure location and reason, manual-assistance details, ODD compliance, and free-text comments about weather and operating conditions.

The records are distributed across several weekly worksheets, but they share the same flat structure. Each row represents one observed mission attempt. Unlike the automatic dataset, the manual log does not separate transfers, missions, and incidents into linked levels. It also lacks common identifiers such as \texttt{transfer\_id} and \texttt{mission\_id}.

The two sources therefore cannot be merged through a direct identifier-based join. I instead identify corresponding records using temporal and operational attributes, including the execution date, timestamp, mission type, and source and destination positions.

\section{Canonical Data Model}
\label{sec:canonical-data-model}
\subsection{Purpose and Design Objectives}
\label{subsec:purpose-and-design-objectives}
The automatic dataset and the manual field log presented in Section~\ref{sec:availability-of-required-oee-inputs} differ in structure, granularity, identifiers, and information content. The automatic source organises information across transfers, missions, and incidents, whereas the manual source records independent mission observations without shared system identifiers. Moreover, neither source contains all the information required by the proposed OEE framework in a directly usable form.

I therefore developed a canonical data model to provide a unified representation of the required information. The model does not reproduce either source dataset. Instead, it defines a common target structure. The available automatic and manual records are transformed into this structure before being processed by the metrics engine.
The canonical model serves two purposes. In the present implementation, it provides a standard input structure for consolidating the heterogeneous industrial records. It also defines a recommended structure for future data collection within the company. Future systems can record OEE-relevant information directly in this form, reducing the need for reconstruction and manual interpretation.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:canonical_data_model}, the core model consists of six operational entities: \texttt{Transfers}, \texttt{Missions}, \texttt{Downtime\_Events}, \texttt{Speed\_Loss\_Events}, \texttt{Out\_of\_ODD\_Events}, and \texttt{Shift\_Schedule}. The Transfers entity represents the central operational unit, while the supporting entities store information defined at a finer or broader level of granularity.

\subsection{Transfer as the Central Operational Entity}
\label{subsec:transfer-as-the-central-operational-entity}
The \texttt{Transfers} entity forms the centre of the canonical model. A transfer represents a complete transport order performed between a source position and a destination position. It provides a meaningful unit of analysis because each transfer has a defined execution interval, an operational scenario, a final outcome, and a nominal-duration reference.

Transfer records provide the main inputs for Performance and Quality. Actual and nominal durations are used to evaluate execution speed, while the standardised outcome determines whether the transfer contributes to Useful Time. Availability is calculated separately at vehicle-and-operating-day level using \texttt{Shift\_Schedule} and \texttt{Downtime\_Events}. The transfer-level results are then aggregated over the same evaluation period to obtain the complete OEE.

\subsection{Supporting Entities and Relationships}
\label{subsec:supporting-entities-and-relationships}
The supporting entities represent information recorded at a different level from the complete transfer. \texttt{Missions} preserves the operational tasks composing each transfer, while \texttt{Downtime\_Events}, \texttt{Speed\_Loss\_Events}, and \texttt{Out\_of\_ODD\_Events} represent distinct types of operational losses or exclusions. \texttt{Shift\_Schedule} stores the planned operating information defined for each vehicle and operating day.

Each mission is linked to one transfer through \texttt{transfer\_id}. Downtime and speed-loss events may also be linked to a specific transfer. However, this relationship can remain optional because some events may occur between transfers. Similarly, an ODD event may affect a specific transfer or a broader operating interval. It can therefore also be associated with the vehicle and operating date.

\texttt{Shift\_Schedule} is independent of individual transfers because a scheduled operating period exists even when no transfer is executed. It is identified by the combination of \texttt{date} and \texttt{vehicle\_id}. Table~\ref{tab:canonical-entity-granularity} summarises the level of granularity and analytical role of each entity.

\subsection{Role as a Reference Data Structure}
\label{subsec:role-as-a-reference-data-structure}
I discussed the proposed canonical model with members of the engineering team. The discussion focused on its potential use for future data collection and framework development. The model identifies the information that should be recorded systematically to support OEE computation.

This information includes common identifiers, shift schedules, event timestamps, nominal durations, failure categories, repair times, and ODD exclusions. I therefore present the model as a technical recommendation. It does not represent an already deployed company data architecture.

In this work, I first populate the model using the available automatic and manual records. This step tests whether the existing industrial data can be transformed into the proposed structure. It also identifies which fields are directly available, which can be reconstructed, and which require estimates or configuration assumptions.

I then test the model using controlled synthetic data. These datasets contain complete and predefined inputs. They make it possible to evaluate the data structure and calculation engine under known operating conditions and loss scenarios.

The two datasets serve different purposes. The industrial data demonstrates the feasibility of applying the proposed model to existing records. The synthetic data supports controlled validation of its completeness, consistency, and suitability for OEE computation.



\section{Framework Summary}
The proposed framework extends the classical \gls{oee} methodology to the evaluation of autonomous yard logistics systems while preserving the original structure. The principal adaptations introduced in this work include the definition of the transfer as the measurement unit, the extension of the time hierarchy to account for autonomous operation, the explicit treatment of Operational Design Domain (ODD) exclusions, and the introduction of autonomous-vehicle-specific loss classifications. Figure~\ref{fig:framework_summary} presents an overview of the adopted framework.
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/frameworksummary.png}
  \caption{Overview of the proposed OEE-based performance evaluation framework}
  \label{fig:framework_summary}

Availability, Performance, and Quality remain the primary measures used to compute OEE, GEE, and TEEP, whereas complementary indicators support diagnosis of operational performance without altering the OEE calculation itself.

Reliability metrics (MTBF, MTTR, and failure rate) characterise the technical behaviour of the autonomous vehicle, while process-quality indicators monitor the robustness of mission execution. Process capability indices (Cp and Cpk) evaluate the consistency of execution times, and operational indicators such as intervention rate, supervision workload, throughput, and weather correlation provide additional information regarding deployment readiness.

The framework definition is complemented by an assessment of the available industrial inputs and by the specification of a canonical data model. Together, these additions connect the conceptual OEE structure to the data architecture required for implementation. 

\section{Conclusion}
In this chapter, I developed an OEE-based framework for autonomous yard logistics. I retained the classical relationship between Availability, Performance, and Quality. I then adapted the quantities used to calculate these factors to the operational characteristics of autonomous transport.

I selected the transfer as the primary measurement unit and developed a six-level time hierarchy. I also decomposed Performance into dispatch utilisation and execution speed. This decomposition separates vehicle underuse from delays during mission execution. In addition, I introduced an ODD-aware time-accounting method that distinguishes external operating restrictions from technical unavailability.

I defined overlap-correction rules and a transfer-outcome taxonomy for autonomous operations. I also incorporated complementary indicators for reliability, human supervision, throughput, environmental influence, process quality, and execution-time capability. Together, these indicators provide additional diagnostic information without changing the core OEE calculation.

Finally, I assessed the availability of the industrial inputs required by the framework. I identified the main gaps in the source data and defined a canonical data model containing transfers, missions, events, ODD exclusions, and shift information. This model aligns the available data with the proposed time hierarchy and OEE factors.

These contributions adapt classical OEE to the operational characteristics of autonomous yard logistics. \textbf{The resulting framework links the conceptual OEE model to the data structure required for implementation and validation.} The following chapter presents the implementation, validation, and evaluation of the framework. It covers data integration, event classification, nominal-duration estimation, ODD integration, KPI computation, and controlled validation.
