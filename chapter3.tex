\section{Introduction}

The previous chapter defined the proposed \gls{oee} framework for autonomous yard logistics. This chapter presents how I implemented and validated the framework. The implementation combines a \textbf{transfer-level \gls{oee} engine with a mission-phase layer for more detailed operational analysis}.

The implementation uses the canonical data model defined in the previous chapter and applies it to the available industrial records through a structured ingestion pipeline. However, due to incomplete and heterogeneous records, the resulting indicators are treated as illustrative rather than as a definitive evaluation of vehicle performance. \textbf{Controlled synthetic scenarios were therefore developed to validate the calculation logic, sensitivity, and consistency of the framework.}

The chapter first presents the implementation components: the data integration and model-ingestion pipeline, weather-data processing for \gls{odd} integration, the transfer-level metrics and reporting engine, and the mission-phase diagnostic extension. It then presents the validation approach, the controlled synthetic validation results, and the mission-phase diagnostic validation.

\section{Data Integration and Model Ingestion Pipeline}
\label{sec:data-integration-and-model-ingestion-pipeline}

The automatic dataset export and manual field log were transformed into the canonical model defined in Section~\ref{sec:canonical-data-model} through a three-stage pipeline. The process first harmonises and cross-matches the two sources, then reconstructs complete transfers and consolidates the available information, and finally populates the canonical entities and derives the missing \gls{oee} inputs. \subsection{Source Harmonisation and Automatic-Manual Cross-Matching}
\label{subsec:source-harmonisation-and-automatic-manual-cross-matching}
The automatic and manual datasets use different identifiers, timestamp formats, and levels of precision. Destination identifiers were therefore normalised into a common format, while date and time fields were converted into comparable timestamps.

Records were matched using execution date, mission type, destination position, and start time. Date, mission type, and destination had to match exactly after normalisation, while a maximum start-time difference of 30 min was allowed. A one-to-one rule was applied so that each automatic mission could be associated with only one manual observation. The matching criteria are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:auto-manual-matching-rules}.

The procedure was applied to \textbf{750} automatic missions and \textbf{944} manual attempts. It produced 101 matched pairs. The matched dataset preserves the fields from both sources and records the time difference used during the association.

\subsection{Transfer Reconstruction and Dataset Consolidation}
\label{subsec:transfer-reconstruction-and-dataset-consolidation}
Matched and unmatched records from Section~\ref{subsec:source-harmonisation-and-automatic-manual-cross-matching} are reconstructed into transfer-level observations and consolidated into a common intermediate dataset.

For the automatic source, the existing transfer structure is preserved, with missions and incidents linked through the original system identifiers. When a manual match is available, its contextual information is attached to the same operation.

Manual observations require transfer reconstruction because no transfer identifier is available. Consecutive mission attempts are grouped when they occur on the same day and the gap between them does not exceed 15 min. A reconstructed transfer generally consists of a pick-up followed by a set-down, with an optional parking movement.

Matched records enrich overlapping observations, while unmatched records from both sources are retained when they contain usable information. After standardisation of timestamps, positions, operating mode, and data origin, the consolidated dataset contains \textbf{908} transfers and \textbf{1,692 missions}, including \textbf{101 matched automatic–manual pairs}.

A \texttt{Shift\_Schedule} record is generated for each observed vehicle and operating day.


\subsection{Derivation and Configuration of Missing OEE Inputs}
\label{subsec:derivation-and-configuration-of-missing-oee-inputs}
Several inputs required by the OEE framework are not directly available in the industrial records. I therefore complete the calculation chain using three approaches. Some values are configured from project information. Others are derived from the observed data. When no measurement is available, I use a documented assumption. Table~\ref{tab:configured-derived-oee-inputs} summarises these inputs and their role in the implementation.

I define the operating schedule from project documentation. The planned shift runs \textbf{from 07:00 to 13:00, Monday to Friday}. Public holidays are excluded from the planned operating period. I also introduce startup and refuelling durations as planned exclusions when calculating Required Time. No software or firmware update events were recorded during the analysed period. I therefore set their duration to zero and assume that they occur outside the operating shift.

Nominal transfer durations require a separate treatment because the source data contains no authoritative reference duration. I therefore define a scenario-specific \textbf{provisional operational benchmark} from successful transfers completed without errors or retries. This benchmark is not presented as a universal ideal cycle time. For each scenario, I set it to 1.20 times the minimum duration observed among these clean transfers:

$$
t_{\mathrm{nominal},s} = 1.20 \times \min_{i \in C_s} \left(t_{\mathrm{actual},i}\right)
$$

Here, $C_s$ represents the set of clean successful transfers for scenario $s$. The multiplier of 1.20 was selected as a transparent margin above the observed best-case clean execution, rather than as a universal engineering constant. It acknowledges that the shortest observation may be unusually fast and provides a reproducible reference in the absence of an independently specified cycle time. Consequently, ESR and all indicators based on this duration should be interpreted as conditional on this benchmark definition. A formal sensitivity analysis over alternative multipliers and validation against an authoritative industrial reference remain necessary before using the values for contractual or cross-site comparison. When the available reference observations are insufficient, I use a fallback nominal value of \textbf{120 s}. This fallback affects \textbf{37} transfers, corresponding to \textbf{4.1\%} of the consolidated dataset. I also apply a transfer-duration cap of 7,200 s as an outlier-control rule. This prevents abnormally long transfers from distorting the Execution Speed Rate.

Other quantities also require substitute values. Manual emergency-stop events have no recorded duration. I therefore assign \textbf{30 s} to each event. This concerns three downtime events. Repair duration is also missing for part of the dataset. When no measured repair interval is available, I use a value of \textbf{5 min} per failure. This affects \textbf{268} transfers and is particularly relevant to the manual records.

I apply two additional limits during reconstruction. Recovery gaps are capped at 8 h. This prevents overnight periods from being interpreted as downtime. Manual mission attempts are grouped into the same reconstructed transfer only when the gap between consecutive operations does not exceed \textbf{15 min}.

Together, these configured, derived, and fallback values complete the input set required by the canonical model described in Section~\ref{sec:canonical-data-model}.

\section{Weather Data Processing and ODD Integration}
\label{sec:weather-data-processing-and-odd-integration}
The \gls{odd}-accounting method defined in Section~\ref{sec:odd-exclusion-framework} requires environmental conditions to be converted into time-based exclusions that can be used by the metrics engine. This section describes how this process was implemented. Weather observations are first prepared at hourly resolution, then compared with the configured \gls{odd} limits. Violating periods are finally converted into \texttt{Out\_of\_ODD\_Events} and integrated into the daily operating schedule.

\subsection{Weather Data Acquisition and Preparation}
\label{subsec:weather-data-acquisition-and-preparation}
Meteorological data was obtained from the \textbf{ERA5 hourly single-level reanalysis dataset} provided through the Copernicus Climate Data Store~\cite{era5_single_levels_cds}. ERA5 combines numerical model information with observations to provide a spatially and temporally consistent representation of atmospheric conditions. The selected product provides hourly data on a global regular latitude--longitude grid with a spatial resolution of $0.25^\circ \times 0.25^\circ$.

ERA5 was used because no local weather station provided continuous measurements for the analysed period. The dataset therefore provides a consistent external source for reconstructing the environmental conditions corresponding to the available operational records.

For the present implementation, the available weather archives cover December 2022 to April 2023. The grid point closest to the operating site was selected at $48.50^\circ\text{N},\,10.25^\circ\text{E}$. The required atmospheric variables were extracted and converted into the units used by the ODD-classification procedure.

The converted weather observations are then aligned with the operating schedule. Only weather records corresponding to a scheduled operating interval are relevant for \gls{oee} computation. Conditions outside this period do not generate an \gls{odd} exclusion.

\subsection{Hourly Weather-ODD Classification}
\label{subsec:hourly-weather-odd-classification}
Each retained hourly observation is evaluated against the configurable weather limits stored in \texttt{ODD\_Parameters}. Five environmental conditions are considered: heavy rain, snowfall, strong wind, freezing temperature, and fog.

An hour is classified as outside the weather ODD when at least one of these conditions exceeds its corresponding limit. When several conditions occur simultaneously, the hour is excluded only once. A precedence rule may be used to assign a dominant cause for reporting, but it does not increase the excluded duration.

The fog condition is implemented as a proxy because no direct fog variable is used in the processing pipeline. A small difference between air temperature and dew-point temperature, combined with high cloud cover, is used to identify atmospheric conditions associated with reduced visibility.

The threshold values are stored as configuration parameters rather than embedded directly in the processing logic. They can therefore be modified without changing the weather-processing workflow.

\subsection{ODD Event Generation and Canonical-Model Integration}
\label{subsec:odd-event-generation-and-canonical-model-integration}
After hourly classification, the violating records are aggregated by operating day. The weather-based exclusion time is calculated from the proportion of observed shift hours classified as outside the ODD:

$$
T_{\mathrm{ODD,wx}} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{bad}}}{N_{\mathrm{observed}}} \, T_{\mathrm{shift}},
$$

where $N_{\mathrm{bad}}$ is the number of violating hourly observations, $N_{\mathrm{observed}}$ is the number of available weather observations within the corresponding operating period, and $T_{\mathrm{shift}}$ is the scheduled shift duration.

The proportional calculation allows the procedure to remain applicable when the available weather records do not cover the complete operating window.

This integration connects the weather-processing module directly to the canonical model defined in Section~\ref{sec:canonical-data-model}. The metrics engine therefore receives the ODD exclusion through the same standardised structure as the other OEE inputs. Its treatment within the $T_o \rightarrow T_r$ transition follows the methodology already defined in Section~\ref{sec:odd-exclusion-framework}.

\section{Transfer-Level Metrics and Reporting Engine}
\label{sec:transfer-level-metrics-and-reporting-engine}
The transfer-level metrics engine is the analytical core of the framework. It takes the canonical dataset produced by the integration pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:data-integration-and-model-ingestion-pipeline}), enriched with ODD information (Section~\ref{sec:weather-data-processing-and-odd-integration}), and applies the calculation rules from Chapter 2 to derive the six-level time hierarchy, the OEE factors, and the complementary operational indicators. This section presents the resulting analysis based on the consolidated industrial dataset.
\subsection{Time-Hierarchy Computation}
\label{subsec:time-hierarchy-computation}
The engine computes the six time levels sequentially:

$$
T_t \rightarrow T_o \rightarrow T_r \rightarrow T_a \rightarrow T_n \rightarrow T_u.
$$

Their mathematical definitions were established in Section~\ref{sec:six-level-time-hierarchy} and are therefore not repeated here. The implementation applies these definitions directly to the consolidated dataset.

The analysed period covers \textbf{95 calendar days}, corresponding to \textbf{2,280.00 h} of Calendar Time. The populated shift schedule contains 44 vehicle-shift entries, representing \textbf{264.00 h} of Planned Operating Time. After the configured planned exclusions and ODD-related periods are removed, \textbf{139.40 h} remain as Required Time.

The effective unplanned downtime is subsequently deducted to obtain 79.56 h of Available Time. The nominal durations associated with qualifying started transfers produce 17.20 h of Performance-Adjusted Time. Finally, the nominal contribution of autonomously completed transfers results in 10.16 h of Useful Time. These values are consistent with the time hierarchy used by the current metrics implementation.

\subsection{OEE Factor Computation}
\label{subsec:oee-factor-computation}
The time hierarchy is used to calculate the three OEE factors. For the consolidated dataset, Availability is:

$$
A = \frac{T_a}{T_r} = \frac{79.56}{139.40} = 57.08\%.
$$

Performance is:

$$
P = \frac{T_n}{T_a} = \frac{17.20}{79.56} = 21.62\%.
$$

The time-based Quality factor is:

$$
Q = \frac{T_u}{T_n} = \frac{10.16}{17.20} = 59.08\%.
$$

The resulting Overall Equipment Effectiveness is:

$$
\mathrm{OEE} = \frac{T_u}{T_r} = 7.29\%.
$$

The factor formulation provides the corresponding internal consistency check:

$$
\mathrm{OEE} = A \times P \times Q = 57.08\% \times 21.62\% \times 59.08\% = 7.29\%.
$$

The engine also reports a count-based Quality indicator. For the consolidated dataset, this value is 60.41\%. The difference from the time-based value arises because the nominal duration varies between transfer scenarios; consequently, a transfer does not necessarily contribute the same amount of nominal time as another.

A mission-phase diagnostic extension was therefore added using the MP1--MP8 sequence defined in Table~\ref{tab:mission-phases}.

The industrial records do not contain reliable timestamps for each mission phase. The phase-level dataset is therefore reconstructed synthetically from the observed transfer and mission records.

The resulting indicators are used for diagnostic demonstration only. They do not represent directly measured phase-level performance. The transfer-level OEE remains unchanged.

\subsection{Construction of the Synthetic Mission-Phase Dataset}
\label{subsec:construction-of-the-synthetic-mission-phase-dataset}
The industrial logs contain transfer and mission durations but do not contain phase boundaries. The \texttt{Mission\_phases} records must therefore be reconstructed.

For each eligible transfer, the recorded scenario is first identified. The corresponding mission and phase sequence is then obtained from the reference configuration. The MP1--MP8 sequences were introduced earlier in Table~\ref{tab:mission-phases} and are therefore not repeated here. A nominal duration is first assigned to each phase using the rules defined in Section~\ref{subsec:mission-phase-reference-and-nominal-durations}. However, these nominal values represent reference execution times and do not reproduce the duration observed in the industrial transfer.

To construct the phase-level actual durations, the recorded duration of the parent transfer is distributed across its phases. First, the nominal durations of all phases belonging to the transfer are summed. A scaling factor is then calculated as:

$$
\alpha = \frac{T_{\mathrm{actual},\mathrm{transfer}}}{\sum_j T_{\mathrm{nominal},j}}.
$$

Each phase nominal duration is then multiplied by this factor:

$$
T_{\mathrm{actual},j} = \alpha \, T_{\mathrm{nominal},j}.
$$

In this way, phases with a larger nominal duration receive a larger share of the observed transfer time. The generated phase durations therefore remain consistent with the duration of their parent transfer.

A small random variation of $\pm 10\%$ is then applied to the generated phase durations. This avoids producing identical execution patterns for phases with the same reference values and introduces controlled variability into the synthetic dataset.

The generator also introduces controlled error and retry behaviour. A failed phase can be followed by another attempt of the same phase. Failed attempts use a duration between 1.0 and 2.0 times their nominal duration.

\subsection{Phase-Level Diagnostic Metrics}
\label{subsec:phase-level-diagnostic-metrics}
The generated mission-phase dataset is used to calculate diagnostic Availability, Performance, and Quality for each phase.

Phase Availability considers only downtime that can be assigned to a specific phase:

$$
A_{\mathrm{phase}} = 1 - \frac{T_{\mathrm{DT},\mathrm{phase}}}{T_{\mathrm{phase}}}.
$$

Downtime that cannot be linked to a phase remains classified as between-phase downtime. It is retained only in the transfer-level calculation.

At mission-phase level, Performance is represented by the Execution Speed Rate (\acrshort{esr}) only:

$$
P_{\mathrm{phase}} = \mathrm{ESR}_{\mathrm{phase}}.
$$

\acrshort{dur} is not used at this level. \acrshort{dur} measures the share of vehicle Available Time occupied by qualifying execution. This denominator is meaningful at transfer and operating-period level, but not for an individual phase. For a short phase, a phase-level \acrshort{dur} would mainly reflect how often that phase occurs during the shift rather than how efficiently it is executed.

\acrshort{esr} is therefore more suitable for phase diagnosis. It directly compares the actual duration of a phase with its nominal reference and allows MP1--MP8 to be compared independently of their frequency or duration within the shift.

The phase-level Quality indicator follows the time-based Quality principle defined in Chapter 2. Availability, \acrshort{esr}, and Quality can then be combined into a diagnostic phase effectiveness value:

$$
\mathrm{OEE}_{\mathrm{phase},\mathrm{diag}} = A_{\mathrm{phase}} \times \mathrm{ESR}_{\mathrm{phase}} \times Q_{\mathrm{phase}}.
$$

This value is used only to compare phases. It is not aggregated to obtain the headline OEE.

Controlled synthetic data are therefore used to complement the industrial application. At transfer level, datasets with predefined operating conditions are generated and processed through the same metrics engine. This makes it possible to compare the calculated Availability, Performance, Quality, and OEE with expected values. At mission-phase level, the reconstructed MP1–MP8 structure is used to examine whether phase-specific losses are reflected in the corresponding diagnostic indicators.

\subsection{Validation Checks}
\label{subsec:validation-checks}
\label{sec:acceptance-tolerance}
For every controlled dataset, the calculated OEE must satisfy the relationship

$$
\mathrm{OEE} = \frac{T_u}{T_r} = A \times P \times Q.
$$

The Performance calculation must also preserve the decomposition

$$
P = \mathrm{DUR} \times \mathrm{ESR}.
$$

The difference between the equivalent calculation paths is required to remain below $10^{-6}$.

The calculated time levels must also preserve the six-level hierarchy

$$
T_t \geq T_o \geq T_r \geq T_a \geq T_n \geq T_u \geq 0,
$$

and the three OEE factors must remain within

$$
A, P, Q \in [0,1].
$$

For scenarios generated around predefined numerical targets, a maximum deviation of five percentage points is accepted:

$$
\left| X_{\mathrm{computed}} - X_{\mathrm{target}} \right| \leq 5\ \text{percentage points},
$$

where $X$ represents $A$, $P$, $Q$, or $\mathrm{OEE}$.

\subsection{Factor Sensitivity Check}\label{subsec:factor-sensitivity-check}The three validation profiles evaluate the complete data-generation and computation pipeline at selected operating levels. A one-parameter-at-a-time sensitivity analysis was additionally performed to examine how the individual OEE factors respond to their corresponding losses.

In each test, one loss mechanism was varied from 0\% to 30\%, while the remaining conditions were kept constant. Unplanned downtime was varied for Availability, unused Available Time for Performance, and unsuccessful execution for Quality.

Availability decreases as unplanned downtime increases, Performance decreases as the unused share of Available Time increases, and Quality decreases as unsuccessful execution increases. The remaining factors stay unchanged within each test.


In the Availability and Quality tests, the OEE curve lies slightly below the varied factor because the reference Performance remains marginally below 100\%. Transfers are scheduled as complete operational units, and the remaining Available Time at the end of an operating window may be insufficient to accommodate another complete transfer. In the Performance test, Availability and Quality remain at 100\%, and OEE therefore coincides with Performance.

The sensitivity analysis confirms the expected response of each OEE dimension to its associated loss. Together with the controlled validation profiles, the results show that the implemented framework behaves consistently across different operating levels and across a continuous range of controlled loss conditions.

\section{Mission-Phase Diagnostic Validation}
\label{sec:mission-phase-validation}

Performance, and Quality conditions. The mission-phase layer requires a

records were then evaluated using the phase diagnostic engine introduced in

\subsection{Controlled Phase-Level Datasets}
\label{subsec:controlled-phase-datasets}

The three controlled datasets were expanded using the same phase-reconstruction
procedure. Each generated phase record contains its nominal reference,
reconstructed duration, outcome, and any downtime that can be associated with
that phase.

The resulting datasets contain 16,415, 22,346, and 25,617 mission-phase records for
the OEE-50, OEE-80, and OEE-100 profiles, respectively. These dataset sizes provide
the basis for the phase-level comparisons presented below.

The three datasets therefore provide a controlled progression from a degraded
condition to a nearly ideal operating condition.


\subsection{Phase Availability Response}
\label{subsec:phase-av-validation}

Phase Availability provides a clear diagnostic response to the controlled
degradation. At the OEE-100 level, all phases reach full Availability because
no phase-attributed downtime is generated.

As the overall effectiveness decreases, the largest Availability losses appear in MP2, MP7, and MP8, with an additional reduction in MP5 at the OEE-50 level. MP2 and MP7 show the strongest degradation, reaching 37.70\% and 49.48\%, respectively. MP8 decreases to 73.58%, while MP5 reaches 72.78\%. MP1 remains effectively unaffected, with only a marginal reduction to 99.73\%. MP3, MP4, and MP6 remain at 100\%.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\subsection{Overall Findings and Root-Cause Synthesis}
The implementation demonstrates that the proposed OEE framework can be applied to autonomous yard-logistics operations using heterogeneous industrial records. The automatic data export and manual field logs were harmonised, reconstructed, and mapped to the canonical data model before the OEE indicators were computed. The canonical model therefore provides a common structure between the available operational data and the metrics engine.

The industrial application also confirms the need to separate different types of operational loss. Availability accounts for unplanned interruptions during Required Time, Performance reflects additional execution time relative to the nominal reference, and Quality distinguishes useful autonomous completion from unsuccessful or manually completed execution. The complementary reliability, process-quality, capability, and supervision indicators provide additional diagnostic information without modifying the OEE calculation itself.

The industrial results show the complete calculation chain on real operational records. Some inputs were reconstructed or configured because they were not recorded directly. Longer recordings, more vehicles, and independent reference values are needed to confirm wider performance claims.

The industrial database covered one vehicle and a short recording period. It shows that the framework can be applied to the available records. It does not establish fleet-wide or long-term performance. For this dataset, Availability was \textbf{57.08\%}, Performance \textbf{21.62\%}, Quality \textbf{59.08\%}, and OEE \textbf{7.29\%} across \textbf{908 transfers} and \textbf{1,692 missions}. The results show the main losses: system faults accounted for \textbf{69.73\%} of downtime, recovery gaps for \textbf{24.02\%}, and the low Performance value was mainly linked to an Execution Speed Rate of \textbf{21.62\%}. Wider conclusions require more vehicles, longer observation periods, and independent reference values.

The controlled synthetic validation confirms that the calculation engine works under known conditions. The degraded, operational, and high-effectiveness profiles produced OEE values close to their targets: \textbf{50.29\%}, \textbf{79.93\%}, and \textbf{98.49\%}. The sensitivity analysis also showed that changes in Availability, Performance, and Quality affect the correct factors. These results validate the calculations. They do not prove real-world improvement.

The mission-phase extension adds a second level of analysis. Transfer-level OEE provides the overall effectiveness of the transport operation, while phase-level Availability, ESR, Quality, and diagnostic phase effectiveness localise losses within the MP1--MP8 sequence. The two levels are therefore complementary: the transfer level indicates how much effectiveness is lost, while the phase level helps identify where the loss occurs.

The different factors identified throughout the analysis are summarised in the Ishikawa diagram shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ishikawa_effectiveness}. The diagram groups the potential causes of reduced operational effectiveness into six categories: vehicle and autonomy behaviour, mission execution, yard and traffic interaction, human supervision, operational coordination, and environmental conditions.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/Ishikawa.png}
    \caption{Root-cause synthesis of factors affecting operational effectiveness in autonomous yard logistics.}
    \label{fig:ishikawa_effectiveness}
\end{figure}
The synthesis shows that reduced effectiveness cannot be attributed to a single technical cause. System faults and recovery-related events mainly contribute to Availability losses. Protective stops, manoeuvring delays, and other increases in execution time affect Performance. Retries and unsuccessful mission execution contribute to Quality losses. Human supervision, yard interactions, operational coordination, and ODD restrictions may influence one or more dimensions. The proposed framework combines these views by quantifying their global effect at transfer level and supporting a finer diagnosis at mission-phase level.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Data Requirements}
The main limitations can be divided into three categories: data completeness, telemetry and measurement, and loss classification.

\subsubsection{Data Completeness}
The main limitation of the industrial evaluation is the \textbf{quality and completeness of the available data}. The automatic data export and manual field logs were produced independently and differ in structure, identifiers, temporal precision, and acquisition periods. As a result, only part of the observations could be matched directly, while the remaining records required source-specific reconstruction.

This limitation is consistent with the purpose of the industrial database: it was made available to test the methodology, not to evaluate operational performance. Consequently, the reported indicators demonstrate that the calculation chain can be executed on real operational records, but they cannot by themselves establish a statistically reliable improvement in availability, performance, or quality.

Several inputs required by the OEE framework were also unavailable as direct measurements. The operating schedule and planned exclusions were configured from project information, while nominal transfer durations were derived from successful executions. In particular, the 1.20 multiplier applied to the shortest clean transfer is a provisional benchmark choice, not an independently validated ideal cycle time. The resulting ESR, Performance-Adjusted Time, Useful Time, and capability indices are therefore conditional on this reference definition. Some repair and event durations were also missing and therefore required fallback values. These assumptions make the calculation possible, but they also introduce uncertainty into the results. Future validation should compare the benchmark with engineering cycle-time specifications and report sensitivity results for alternative multipliers, such as 1.10 and 1.30.

The environmental analysis has a similar limitation. Weather conditions were reconstructed from ERA5 hourly data using the closest available grid point rather than measurements collected directly at the operating yard. The resulting weather-based ODD exclusions should therefore be considered an approximation of the actual environmental conditions experienced by the vehicle.

The mission-phase diagnostic layer is further limited by the absence of reliable timestamps for MP1--MP8 in the industrial records. Phase durations were therefore reconstructed using transfer and mission information together with configured nominal references. The phase indicators demonstrate the diagnostic potential of the framework, but they should not be interpreted as directly measured phase performance.

\subsubsection{Telemetry and Measurement}
The framework depends strongly on telemetry data. Telemetry provides the timestamps, states, durations, and event signals used to reconstruct the time hierarchy and the OEE factors. Data quality problems can therefore affect the results. These problems include missing messages, delayed transmission, unsynchronised clocks, coarse sampling, and changes in the meaning of status codes.

The framework measures what can be observed through the telemetry system. It cannot identify losses that are not recorded or cannot be inferred. This dependence limits comparisons between different deployments unless the data schema, timestamp rules, sampling resolution, and event definitions are standardised and carefully controlled.

\subsubsection{Loss Classification}
Loss classification is another important limitation. Some events cannot be assigned to a single category directly. For example, a single event may be related to technical downtime, an autonomy limitation, or an ODD exclusion. The same event may also affect more than one mission phase.

The proposed precedence and overlap-correction rules make the calculation more consistent, but engineering judgement is still required when defining event mappings. The classification system may also need to change as the system evolves. New failure codes, intervention modes, or operating procedures may require a revised taxonomy.

A stable classification dictionary is therefore needed. Clear event precedence rules should also be defined, and regular review with operations and maintenance experts is recommended.

\subsubsection{Future Data Requirements}
Future implementations should prioritise the direct and consistent recording of the inputs required by the proposed data model. The following information should be prioritised:

\begin{itemize}
    \item persistent transfer and mission identifiers
    \item complete shift schedules and planned stoppages
    \item synchronised timestamps
    \item technical-failure and recovery timestamps
    \item structured ODD events
    \item start and end timestamps for each mission phase
\end{itemize}

Direct recording of these data would reduce the need for reconstruction and support continuous operational monitoring rather than only retrospective analysis.

\subsection{Transferability to Other Environments}
The framework is designed around concepts that can extend beyond the studied yard, including transfer-based measurement, hierarchical time accounting and ODD exclusions. It can therefore provide a useful starting point for other autonomous transport environments, such as different logistics yards, warehouses, or industrial sites. However, the current implementation should not be considered universally applicable without adaptation.

Portability requires recalibration of the mission and transfer definitions, nominal durations, outcome taxonomy and ODD thresholds. A warehouse may be dominated by congestion and pedestrian interactions, whereas a port may introduce different traffic rules, loading constraints, and weather limits. The meaning of a successful transfer, an acceptable intervention, or an excluded operating period may therefore differ between environments. In addition, a framework validated with one vehicle, one telemetry architecture, and one site-specific dataset cannot by itself establish validity for another fleet or operating context.

Extension to other environments should therefore follow a staged process: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item define the local operational concepts and ODD criteria
    \item map available telemetry to the canonical data model
    \item calibrate nominal references and classification rules with domain experts
    \item validate the resulting indicators against independently observed operational outcomes
\end{itemize}
\section{Conclusion}
This chapter presented the implementation and validation of the proposed OEE framework for autonomous yard logistics. A structured ingestion pipeline was developed to transform heterogeneous industrial records into the canonical data model. Weather-based ODD information was then integrated before computing transfer-level OEE, complementary engineering indicators, and mission-phase diagnostic metrics.

Application to the consolidated industrial data demonstrated that the complete calculation chain can be executed using the proposed structure, while also revealing the effect of incomplete data on the interpretation of the results. \textbf{Controlled synthetic validation confirmed the consistency and sensitivity of the transfer-level calculations}, while the mission-phase analysis demonstrated the ability of the diagnostic extension to localise Availability, Performance, and Quality losses within the operational sequence.

Overall, the proposed framework combines a global measure of operational effectiveness with finer engineering diagnosis. More complete and directly measured operational data are required before the resulting indicators can be interpreted as definitive deployment-level performance measures. The main contribution is therefore a \textbf{structured and practical framework for measuring and diagnosing autonomous yard-logistics performance}.
